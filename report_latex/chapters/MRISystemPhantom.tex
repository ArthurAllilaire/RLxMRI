\section{Chapter aim}
\label{sec:twin-aim}

This chapter delivers A1 and lays the ground for C1: it builds
\texttt{MRISystemPhantom.jl}, a digital twin of the QalibreMD Model 130
calibration phantom that supplies the ground-truth tissue parameters,
geometry, and randomisation the reinforcement-learning loop trains against.
The hardest engineering problem is the background water, which is roughly
80\% of the spins and therefore dominates simulation cost while contributing
little to the per-sphere measurement the agent cares about; the chapter's
main novelty over a fixed phantom model is an independent, coarsenable water
grid (Section~\ref{background-water}) that later enables the multi-fidelity
cost savings of Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl}. In the terminology of
Section~\ref{sec:bg-twins-phantoms}, the result is a bounded
calibration-object twin rather than a patient twin;
Section~\ref{sec:phantom-related-tools} positions it against existing open
phantom hardware and analysis tooling. The remaining sections cover
the geometry and pose model (Sections~\ref{geometry}--\ref{augmentation}),
the calibrated material tables (Section~\ref{material-tables}), the
$T_1$ sequence and fitter the experiments use
(Section~\ref{shipped-sequences-and-fitting-models}), and how the twin plugs
into KomaMRI (Section~\ref{komamri-integration}).

\hypertarget{the-physical-phantom}{%
\section{The physical phantom}\label{the-physical-phantom}}

The QalibreMD NIST/ISMRM
\href{https://qmri.com/product/premium-system-phantom/}{System Standard
Model 130} (manufactured by \href{https://qmri.com}{Caliber MRI},
formerly QalibreMD) is a hardware phantom designed to mimic the geometry
of a human head and to provide a wide, calibrated range of quantitative
MRI tissue parameters (T1, T2, and proton density). It is the standard
reference device for quantitative MRI system validation in clinical and
research settings, and its design is described in the \emph{QalibreMD
System Standard Model 130 User Manual}
(\href{https://github.com/ArthurAllilaire/RLxMRI/blob/main/QalibreMD_NIST_ISMRM_System_Standard_Model_130_T1_T2_PD_User_Manual.pdf}{available
here}). It is the commercial implementation of the ISMRM/NIST
system-phantom standardisation effort introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:bg-twins-phantoms}: its contrast spheres carry
SI-traceable reference values used for scanner characterisation and
multi-site qMRI
comparison~\cite{russek_system_phantom_characterization_2020,keenan_phantoms_2024},
so the digital twin inherits a recognised calibration target rather than a
bespoke test object.

The phantom consists of a spherical water-filled housing (100 mm
internal radius) containing four internal structures at fixed axial
positions:

\section{Related phantom tooling}
\label{sec:phantom-related-tools}

The qMRI community already publishes open artefacts around this phantom at
two layers. At the hardware layer, NIST releases CAD/STL design files for an
open-source MRI phantom, and the ISMRM/NIST system-phantom repository ships
the device's hardware design files, making the physical reference object
itself reproducible.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/usnistgov/open-source-mri-phantom};
\url{https://github.com/MRIStandards/SystemPhantom}.} At the analysis layer,
PhantomViewer provides a Python GUI with virtual-phantom descriptions for
manual ROI analysis of acquired NIST phantom images~\cite{phantomviewer_repo},
and MR-BIAS automates that workflow --- ROI detection plus
Levenberg--Marquardt fits of inversion-recovery and variable-flip-angle
$T_1$ models and multi-echo $T_2$ models --- reducing inter-observer
variability and analysis time on acquired system-phantom
images~\cite{korte_mrbias_2023}.

Both layers stop at the physical scan: one makes the hardware reproducible,
the other analyses images after acquisition. Neither generates simulated
acquisitions, and the MR-BIAS fitting models could not be reused in the
adaptive setting of Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl}, where the agent changes
the timing block by block: the fitter must accept per-block TR and
excitation angle and model the finite-$N_\mathrm{pe}$ transient across
phase-encode shots
(Section~\ref{shipped-sequences-and-fitting-models}), whereas MR-BIAS fits
one fixed protocol per acquired image series.
\texttt{MRISystemPhantom.jl} is the complementary simulator-side layer: it
represents the phantom \emph{before} acquisition as a KomaMRI object with
ground-truth material labels and randomisation hooks, and embeds the fitting
model inside the simulation/RL loop so it can generate synthetic
measurements and rewards for sequence-design experiments.

\hypertarget{design-goals-and-architecture}{%
\section{Design goals and
architecture}\label{design-goals-and-architecture}}

A digital twin of this phantom is needed for two reasons. First, it
provides a ground-truth environment for RL training: the agent needs to
query a simulator that returns physically meaningful signals and can
report the true tissue parameters for reward computation. The material
tables (Section~\ref{material-tables}) and the geometry/sequence/fitter
pipeline (Sections~\ref{geometry} and
\ref{shipped-sequences-and-fitting-models}) address this point. Second, it
must be \textbf{flexible}: the RL training loop needs to randomise pose,
inject noise, and jitter reference values across episodes without
modifying library code, and different experiments require different phantom
sections or resolution. The configuration contract below and the
augmentation/random-phantom machinery (Section~\ref{augmentation}) address
this second point.

The library is structured around a single contract: a
\texttt{PhantomConfig} struct that carries the builder configuration.
A single function, \texttt{build\_phantom}, returns a
\texttt{KomaMRI.Phantom}, which can then be fed directly to
\texttt{KomaMRI.simulate}. This design was chosen deliberately over a
mutable phantom object or a collection of keyword arguments as the
config is serialisable, diffable, and reproducible (the RNG seed is
embedded), and the builder has no hidden state. This is useful for
comparing different RL runs and also simplifies the Python-Julia
boundary used by the training code.

The pipeline from \texttt{PhantomConfig} to \texttt{KomaMRI.simulate()}
is:

The two-stage design --- descriptors first, voxels second --- means all
geometry and material decisions happen before any memory is allocated
for spin arrays. Descriptors are also individually modifiable and
removable: you can simulate only a subset of the 14 spheres per plate,
with the water housing automatically filling the vacated volume. Because
each descriptor carries its physical centre coordinate,
\texttt{sphere\_descriptor\_pixels} can convert those centres directly
to pixel indices in the reconstructed image --- using the same
DC-at-centre convention as \texttt{kspace\_to\_image} --- simplifying
ROI extraction after reconstruction.

\hypertarget{geometry}{%
\section{Geometry}\label{geometry}}

The geometry implementation is judged by whether it preserves the
reconstructed sphere measurements, not by whether every background-water
spin is represented exactly.

\hypertarget{sphere-voxelisation}{%
\subsection{Sphere voxelisation}\label{sphere-voxelisation}}

Each sphere is voxelised on a cubic grid at spacing
\texttt{delta\_x\ =\ voxel\_size\_mm\ *\ 1e-3} metres. A spin is
included if its grid point falls within the sphere radius:

\[
(x_i - c_x)^2 + (y_i - c_y)^2 + (z_i - c_z)^2 \leq r^2
\]

The grid is constructed by iterating over the bounding box
\texttt{{[}c\ -\ r,\ c\ +\ r{]}} on each axis. For a typical 15 mm
radius sphere at 2 mm voxels this produces around 500 spins per sphere.
Across all three contrast plates (42 spheres), the combined spin count
at 2 mm voxels is \textasciitilde9,100, occupying \textasciitilde570 KB
(8 Float64 fields per spin:
\(x, y, z, \rho, T_1, T_2, T_2^*, \Delta\omega\)), small enough that the
full set fits comfortably in memory and the per-step simulate call is
fast.

\hypertarget{slicing}{%
\subsection{Slicing}\label{slicing}}

For 2D imaging experiments, only spins within a thin axial slab need to
be simulated, reducing the spin count, and therefore simulation time,
substantially. A \texttt{Slab} is defined by a centre point
\(\mathbf{c}\), a unit normal \(\hat{n}\), and a thickness \(t\). A spin
at position \(\mathbf{p}\) is included if its signed distance from the
midplane satisfies:

\[
|(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c}) \cdot \hat{n}| \leq t/2
\]

The normal defaults to \(\hat{z}\) but any orientation is supported. A
0.1 \(\mu\)m tolerance is applied at the boundary to avoid floating-point
rejections of on-edge voxels. At 1 mm voxels, a 5 mm axial slab centred
on the T1 plate retains \textasciitilde33,000 of the full phantom's
\textasciitilde4.2 million spins, a \textbf{\(128\times\) reduction}. This
matches the excited volume of a slice-selective RF pulse without needing
to encode one explicitly.

\hypertarget{pose-transforms}{%
\subsection{Pose transforms}\label{pose-transforms}}

\texttt{apply\_transform!} rotates and translates all spin positions by
the Euler XYZ rotation and translation specified in
\texttt{PhantomConfig.rotation} and \texttt{translation\_mm}. The
rotation matrix is built from three successive Rx, Ry, Rz
multiplications. The transform is applied after voxelisation and before
augmentation noise so that the sphere geometry is exact before any
stochastic perturbation. This was designed to make it easy to reset the
phantom and reduce overfitting.

\hypertarget{background-water}{%
\subsection{Background water}\label{background-water}}

The housing is voxelised as a 100 mm-radius sphere with
\texttt{water\_voxel\_size\_mm} spacing (defaulting to
\texttt{voxel\_size\_mm}), with all contrast and fiducial sphere volumes
cut out by the negated form of the squared-distance test used for
voxelisation. The water housing is by far the largest spin population,
yet it is not a quantity the phantom exists to measure, so it is the
natural place to trade fidelity for simulation speed. We do this by
increasing \texttt{water\_voxel\_size\_mm} to coarsen the grid, thus
reducing the spin count. We then reweight each spin's proton density by
\texttt{(water\_dx\ /\ sphere\_dx)³}. This conserves the total water
magnetisation, so the bulk water signal amplitude is correct regardless
of the coarsening factor.

This coarsening must also handle anisotropic resolution as slices are
typically thick (3--8 mm) relative to the in-plane resolution (1--2 mm).
So, when a slice is selected the housing is projected onto a grid of
stacked plane-sheets (\texttt{water\_throughplane\_voxel\_size\_mm}
apart). Again, these are re-weighted to conserve total magnetisation.

The figure below quantifies the fidelity--cost trade for an IR-SE-2D
acquisition.

Conserving the bulk water signal is necessary but not sufficient. The
k-space DC term changes by only 0.35\%, but the phantom is used to
measure the sphere signals. The coarse 3 mm grid uses
\textbf{6,919 vs 23,735 spins} (\(3.4\times\) fewer) and is
\textbf{\textasciitilde\(3.4\times\) faster to simulate}
(\(0.9 \pm 0.1\) s vs \(3.2 \pm 0.6\) s, mean \(\pm\) std over 20
runs). Using the \texttt{sphere\_descriptor\_pixels} mapping, the single
centre-pixel coarse-vs-fine NRMSE at the \(32\times64\) matrix is \textbf{3.7\%}.

The larger single-pixel error reflects where the coarse grid differs
from the fine one. Reweighting preserves the amount of water, but not
the exact water/sphere boundary: the 3 mm water grid stair-steps the
sphere cut-outs, changing high-spatial-frequency edge content while
leaving low-frequency k-space essentially unchanged. The boundary error
is on the scale of the reconstructed pixels here: for a 200 mm FOV and
\(32\times64\) matrix, the spacing is 6.25 mm in phase encode and 3.125 mm in
frequency encode. After band-limited reconstruction, this boundary
mismatch appears as a slightly different Gibbs-ringing pattern around
the spheres.

Most of this error is concentrated in the near-null spheres at this
inversion time, especially T1-spheres 13-14, whose signals move by about
9\%, while the bright spheres change by \(\leq 0.7\%\). This is expected because
the ringing leakage is approximately additive, set mostly by the
surrounding bright water.

If the sphere signal is measured as a small central ROI rather than a
single point sample, the error falls to \textbf{1.5\%} for a \(3\times3\) ROI.
This is the more relevant operational metric for an ROI-based phantom
readout; the single-pixel value remains useful as a worst-case
sensitivity measure because it exposes pixel-phase effects, Gibbs
oscillation, and sub-pixel centre rounding.

The same pattern strengthens at \(64\times64\): with unchanged spin counts and DC
term, the errors rise to \textbf{7.6\%} for a single centre pixel and
\textbf{2.3\%} for the \(3\times3\) ROI, consistent with a wider k-space window
admitting more of the edge mismatch.

Because the discrepancy is carried by truncation side-lobes, it can be
suppressed at reconstruction. The library provides a 2-D Hamming window
through \texttt{hamming\_\allowbreak window\_\allowbreak 2d} and
\texttt{kspace\_\allowbreak to\_\allowbreak image(...;\ hamming=true)}. As derived in the
background chapter, this reduces the PSF side-lobes from -13 dB to -43
dB, at the cost of a wider main lobe.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{imgs/mri_system_phantom/hamming_phantom_diff.png}
\caption{\textbf{Effect of Hamming apodisation on one reconstruction.} A
coarse-water phantom reconstructed without a Hamming window (left), with
a 2-D Hamming window (centre), and their difference (right). Produced by \texttt{examples/hamming\_apodisation.jl}.}
\label{fig:hamming-phantom-diff}
\end{figure}

Reconstructing both grids with the Hamming window reduces the
single-pixel coarse-vs-fine NRMSE by four-fold, from \textbf{3.7\% to
0.9\%}. This confirms that the single-pixel discrepancy was dominated by
truncation ringing. The \(3\times3\)-ROI error improves more modestly, from
\textbf{1.5\% to 1.1\%}, because ROI averaging already cancels much of
the oscillatory side-lobe structure. Apodisation and spatial averaging
therefore suppress largely the same error component, rather than
providing independent gains. The remaining \(\approx 1.1\%\) is the residual
coarse-grid discrepancy under this ROI-based measurement protocol.

The Hamming window down-weights outer k-space, visible as the darkened
periphery of the centre k-space panel in Figure
\ref{fig:hamming-phantom-diff}. This widens the PSF main lobe and
slightly blurs the spheres, but suppresses ringing side-lobes. The
difference is localised to sphere edges and high-frequency k-space,
consistent with suppression of truncation ringing rather than a change
in the smooth low-frequency signal. The window is a reconstruction-side
element-wise multiplication and costs only a fraction of a millisecond,
so it does not affect the roughly \(3\times\) simulation speed-up from
the coarse grid. Its cost is resolution, through the wider PSF main lobe.

A natural future improvement is a boundary-aware water grid: keep the
smooth water bulk coarse, but voxelise a thin shell around the housing
surface and sphere cut-outs at fine resolution so that coarsening no
longer changes the boundary geometry.

The T1 range at 3 T spans two decades: 23 ms to 1.84 s for the NiCl\(_2\)
plate, covering the full range of biological tissue T1 values from white
matter to cerebrospinal fluid. The T2 range spans \textasciitilde87 ms
to 2.76 s. These wide ranges are why the phantom is used for system
validation and make it useful for RL training: to ensure the learned
policies can produce reliable estimates across the full physiological
range.

The \texttt{serial\_number\_class} field switches the T1 table between
current and legacy recipes. All reference values are currently fixed at
\(20^{\circ}\text{C}\); the manual includes temperature-correction
graphs, but these are not yet implemented.

These tables are not merely nominal manufacturer constants. A 2025
multi-centre study measured $T_1$ and $T_2$ in the ISMRM/NIST phantom on 13
scanners across three vendors and seven sites, finding excellent correlation
with the reference values and median inter-scanner coefficients of variation
below 7\%~\cite{pasini_multicenter_nist_t1t2_2025}. This supports using the
tables as a quantitative validation target. The same study tabulates 3 T
reference values at six temperatures between 16 and
\(26^{\circ}\text{C}\), which is why the library records its
\(20^{\circ}\text{C}\) assumption explicitly: temperature correction is the
known gap to close before any simulation-to-real comparison.

\textbf{Accuracy caveats.} Two entries are approximate: PD-plate T1/T2
values (\texttt{pd\_t1}, \texttt{pd\_t2}) use bulk water regardless of
proton-density fraction (the manual does not tabulate per-sphere
relaxation for the PD plate), and fiducial relaxation values
(\texttt{FIDUCIAL\_PROPS}) are generic CuSO\(_4\) placeholders not verified
against any calibration record. Neither affects the T1- or T2-plate
experiments, but both should be replaced before any simulation-to-real
comparison involving the PD plate or fiducial grid.

The T1/T2 jitter is applied at the sphere level (one draw per sphere,
not per spin) to simulate manufacturing variability in the doping
concentration. Of the fields above, only \texttt{T1\_sigma\_rel} and

The \texttt{rng\_seed} is the only source of stochasticity: fixing it
makes \texttt{build\_phantom} deterministic, so the same configuration
always produces the same phantom. Fixing the seed reproduces the exact
same phantom, making evaluation runs reproducible. Incrementing it each
training episode draws a statistically independent phantom, preventing
the agent from memorising a fixed configuration.

\hypertarget{episode-level-random-phantoms}{%
\subsection{Episode-level random phantoms}\label{episode-level-random-phantoms}}

A fixed calibrated phantom is unsuitable for RL training because the
agent can exploit the known sphere ordering. Instead, each episode should
draw a new plausible phantom. Earlier RL code did this manually by editing
sampled \texttt{SphereDescriptor}s and assembling a fresh
\texttt{PhantomConfig}. \texttt{RandomPhantomConfig} makes this explicit:
it is a distribution over deterministic \texttt{PhantomConfig}s. The base
config fixes build settings such as field strength, voxel size, included
plates and water model; the random config specifies what changes between
episodes: active contrast spheres, sampled material values and phantom
pose.

This is distinct from \texttt{AugmentConfig}. \texttt{AugmentConfig}
adds low-level simulation perturbations after the phantom has been
voxelised, such as per-spin off-resonance or position noise. It is useful
for modelling measurement imperfections, but it does not change the
episode's true sphere-level material values. In contrast,
\texttt{RandomPhantomConfig} samples descriptor-level properties before
voxelisation. For example, one training episode might select five T1
spheres, draw one T1 value for each selected sphere from a log-normal
distribution around its nominal value, set T2 by preserving the sphere's
nominal T2/T1 ratio, and then apply a small in-slice rotation and
translation. \texttt{sample\_phantom\_config} returns both the sampled
\texttt{PhantomConfig}, which is safe to pass to \texttt{build\_phantom},
and a hidden truth record used for rewards and evaluation.

The pose sampler is experiment-dependent. Thin-slice acquisitions use
only an in-plane rotation and x/y translation, because a full 3D rotation
would move spheres out of the acquired slab. Volumetric experiments can
instead use \texttt{UniformSO3PoseSampler}, which draws a true uniform
orientation on SO(3) and stores it as a \(3\times3\) rotation matrix. For
this reason, \texttt{PhantomConfig} accepts both Euler-angle triples and
precomputed rotation matrices.

Samplers are explicit objects, using
\texttt{Distributions.\allowbreak jl} where appropriate
\cite{distributions_jl_2021}, so assumptions such as material jitter,
sphere dropout and pose noise are visible rather than hidden in scattered
\texttt{randn} calls. Material distributions can also depend on other
sampled tissue parameters: for example, T1 can be drawn from
\texttt{Uniform(0.3,\ 2.5)} while T2 is defined by
\texttt{PreserveNominalRatio(:T2,\ :T1)}. These property dependencies are
evaluated in order and checked for cycles, so invalid specifications fail
at sampling time. The API remains generic: selectors, material samplers
and pose samplers operate on \texttt{SphereDescriptor}s and
\texttt{PhantomConfig}s, not on RL states, observations or reward code.

\hypertarget{shipped-sequences-and-fitting-models}{%
\section{Shipped sequences and fitting models}\label{shipped-sequences-and-fitting-models}}

All sequence builders return standard \texttt{KomaMRI.\allowbreak Sequence}
objects, and the fitting routines are pure Julia shared by the conventional
baselines and the RL environment, so reported gains are always measured
against the same estimators. The library ships a broader family than this
project exercises --- single-spin and 2-D builders for spin echo
(\texttt{se}), CPMG/multi-echo (\texttt{mse}), turbo spin echo
(\texttt{ir\_tse\_2d}) and spoiled gradient echo (\texttt{gre\_2d}), with
matching \texttt{fit\_t2\_se} and joint \texttt{fit\_t1\_t2\_generalized\_ir}
estimators for the T2 and PD plates --- but the experiments in this report
use only the $T_1$ path, so this section describes that path and leaves the
rest to the package documentation.

The $T_1$ acquisition is \texttt{ir\_se\_2d\_sequence(TI, TE, TR; ...)}: an
inversion-recovery spin echo parameterised by excitation angle
$\alpha_\mathrm{exc}$, \texttt{FOV}, \texttt{Nfe} and \texttt{Npe}, acquiring
one k-space row per shot. The matching estimator is
\texttt{fit\_t1\_generalized\_ir}, which accepts per-block inversion and
excitation angles, optional \texttt{TRs}, and \texttt{Npe}: passing
\texttt{Npe = nothing} selects the fully relaxed steady-state model, while an
integer \texttt{Npe} selects the transient mean over the acquired
phase-encode shots, accounting for the ramp from thermal equilibrium when
TR is short. Both estimators are log-grid scans rather than gradient fits:
\texttt{fit\_t1\_generalized\_ir} evaluates 300 log-spaced $T_1$ candidates
over $[5\times10^{-3}, 5]$~s (the long-TR \texttt{fit\_t1\_ir} uses 400, the
joint $T_1/T_2$ fit a $200\times120$ grid) and keeps the lowest-SSE candidate
with a closed-form amplitude at each. The ${\approx}2.3\%$ spacing between
candidates sets a ${\approx}{\pm}1.1\%$ quantisation floor on any fitted $T_1$,
so the reported $T_1$ MAPEs cannot fall below ${\approx}1\%$ by construction ---
this is the ``fitter grid floor'' referred to in
Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl}. It and the conventional long-TR \texttt{fit\_t1\_ir} estimator
share the longitudinal forward models
(\texttt{steady\_state\_mz\_at\_excite},
\texttt{transient\_mz\_at\_excite\_npe},
\texttt{transient\_mz\_per\_shot}), which are reused unchanged by the cached
background-water model (Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl}) and the Cramer--Rao
baselines, so the same physics underlies the simulator, the fitter and the
fixed comparators.

All image-producing sequences use the same reconstruction path,
\texttt{raw\_to\_kspace} then \texttt{kspace\_to\_image}, with sphere-centre
ROI extraction through \texttt{sphere\_descriptor\_pixels} sharing the
DC-at-centre convention so no separate image-space mapping is hand-coded in
the experiments. Appendix~\ref{app:fit-model-derivations} gives the fit
derivations that matter for interpreting the estimators.

\subsection{The Cramér--Rao baselines}
\label{sec:crlb-baselines}

The fixed-schedule comparators used in Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl} are not
hand-chosen: they are Cramér--Rao-optimal schedules, and because the bound
recurs throughout that chapter it is worth stating what it is. The
Cramér--Rao lower bound (CRLB) is a floor on the variance of \emph{any}
unbiased estimator of a parameter, set by the measurement design and the noise
alone. For Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$, the information a measurement
at inversion time $\mathrm{TI}_k$ carries about $T_1$ is the squared
sensitivity of the signal to $T_1$, $\sigma^{-2}(\partial s_k/\partial T_1)^2$;
summed over measurements this is the Fisher information $I$, and
$\mathrm{Var}(\hat T_1)\ge I^{-1}$. With an unknown amplitude $A$ as well, $I$
becomes the $2\times2$ Fisher matrix $\sigma^{-2}J^\top J$ ($J$ the Jacobian of
the signal in $(T_1, A)$) and the $T_1$ bound is the $(T_1,T_1)$ entry of its
inverse --- larger than the single-parameter value, because the information
must be shared with the nuisance $A$, which is why an informative design needs
at least two well-placed points. The sensitivity $\partial s_k/\partial T_1$
vanishes at very short and very long TI and peaks near $\mathrm{TI}\approx T_1$,
so the most informative inversion times sit on the steep part of the recovery;
Jones et al.~\cite{optimal_sampling_crlb:96} show the exponential optimum is a
few peaky points (one near zero, the rest near $1.28\,T$).

Because the slope is evaluated at a specific $T_1$, the CRLB is optimal
\emph{for an assumed value}. To design one schedule for a whole range,
\texttt{cr\_optimize} minimises the mean relative variance
$M^{-1}\sum_j \mathrm{Var}(\hat T_{1,j})/T_{1,j}^2$ over a fleet of
representative $T_1$ values --- the normalisation makes the amplitude cancel and
weights each $T_1$ fairly --- which is exactly the static-average objective of
Beracha et al.~\cite{adaptive_mri:23}. This objective is non-convex in the block
timings, so it is solved numerically: not by gradient descent or a TI grid, but
by multi-start coordinate descent, scoring random log-uniform schedules and
refining the best by coordinate moves on each $(\mathrm{TI},\mathrm{TR})$ under
the scan-time budget, swept over the number of blocks. Both TI and TR are free
and are evaluated on the same transient forward model as the fitter, so the
solver can trade short, partially-recovered blocks against longer-TR probes
(Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl} reports the resulting schedules and a
seed-stability check).

This bound is also the right lens on the fitter itself:
\texttt{fit\_t1\_generalized\_ir} returns the maximum-likelihood point (the
minimum-SSE grid candidate), which is the peak of the same Gaussian likelihood
whose curvature is the Fisher information --- so the CRLB is the asymptotic
width of the posterior the fitter implicitly maximises. An adaptive scheme that
retained the whole posterior rather than its peak would obtain that width as a
calibrated uncertainty for free (Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl}, future work); the
point fitter must instead bootstrap it.

\hypertarget{komamri-integration}{%
\section{KomaMRI integration}\label{komamri-integration}}

\texttt{MRISystemPhantom.jl} is built entirely on top of
\texttt{KomaMRI.jl} and returns standard \texttt{KomaMRI.\allowbreak Phantom}
objects. The whole phantom is assembled using a single operation: the
concatenation operator \texttt{+} on \texttt{Phantom}. Each voxelised
sphere becomes a small \texttt{Phantom}, the spheres of each T1, T2 and
PD plate are concatenated into that plate, the three plates are
concatenated into a sphere stack, and finally the water background is
concatenated on top. The result is a single array of spins with
heterogeneous relaxation values, which is exactly what
\texttt{KomaMRI.\allowbreak simulate} expects.

Because the output is an ordinary \texttt{KomaMRI.\allowbreak Phantom}, existing
KomaMRI features work out of the box, such as the interactive plotting
tools which are used extensively in the example scripts.

The matching scanner is obtained from \texttt{scanner\_\allowbreak for\_\allowbreak field(cfg)},
which returns a \texttt{Scanner} with B0 set to 3.0 T for \texttt{:T3}
or 1.5 T for \texttt{:T15}. Pairing a phantom with a \texttt{Scanner} of
a different B0 silently produces the wrong relaxation values - a mistake
the author made in practice - so this helper exists to make the coupling
explicit.

\hypertarget{library-availability}{%
\section{Library availability}\label{library-availability}}

\texttt{MRISystemPhantom.jl} is developed as a standalone open-source
Julia package, with source on
\href{https://github.com/ArthurAllilaire/MRISystemPhantom.jl}{GitHub} and
\href{https://arthurallilaire.github.io/MRISystemPhantom.jl}{API
documentation} on GitHub Pages. It ships a complete test suite
(\textasciitilde373 tests) and a set of runnable example scripts covering
phantom construction, $T_1$ mapping, the conventional fixed-sequence
baseline, NEMA MS-1 SNR calibration, and the water-coarsening/apodisation
fidelity checks reported here. Packaging the work as a tested library rather
than experiment-specific scripts is a deliberate contribution: it provides a
reusable, reproducible digital twin of a widely deployed calibration device
that returns standard \texttt{KomaMRI.Phantom} and \texttt{Sequence}
objects, so other groups can check simulated qMRI methods against known
$T_1/T_2$ reference values before scanner deployment.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:twin-limitations}

Beyond the accuracy caveats drawn together in the conclusion
(Chapter~\ref{Conclusions}), the most consequential limitation is the shipped
fitter. It is one-to-one with the sequence family it inverts: each estimator is
a closed-form model for a single signal equation (generalized IR-SE), so every
new acquisition family needs its own bespoke fit, which does not scale. The
estimators are also discrete log-grid scans, so their accuracy is additionally
capped by the grid spacing --- about $1\%$ at the 300 points used in training
and ${\approx}0.5\%$ at 500
(Section~\ref{shipped-sequences-and-fitting-models}). Replacing them with an
EPG-based fitting library would both lift that resolution ceiling and break the
one-to-one coupling, modelling the spin history of echo trains and partial
spoiling across families; integrating a learned deep-learning reconstruction or
an end-to-end fit is the further-out alternative, and needs research.

Several extensions would broaden the twin's reach. Pulseq import/export would
let it drive real scanners and consume the large community bank of
vendor-neutral sequences, instead of the hand-built IR-SE and SE blocks it
generates today, which would also give the RL agent a far larger sequence
action space (Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl}). The voxelisation and
spin-construction logic are currently coupled to KomaMRI; that coupling was
productive here but limits portability, and the voxelisation validated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:validating-komamri} should be exportable to other simulators ---
for example to plug the phantom into MRzero --- reusing the spin logic rather
than re-deriving it. The ROI extraction is sphere-centric; supporting arbitrary,
non-spherical regions would generalise it to other phantom and anatomy
geometries. The water-cache and coarsening fidelity were also validated
narrowly: only on the $T_1$ plate, only for spherical sphere boundaries, with a
Hamming apodisation window, and only for the single thin axial slab used here.
Gibbs ringing depends on boundary shape, the Hamming window is only the most
common of several apodisation kernels, and the cache has not been checked on the
$T_2$/PD plates or for true 3-D acquisitions --- all of which must be confirmed
before relying on it outside this setting.

Finally, the library could better support the RL use case it was built for. Its
visualisation is currently static recovery-curve and image plots; richer tooling
--- episode-averaged parameter choices, or videos of the evolving signal rather
than snapshots --- would make adaptive-policy behaviour far easier to inspect.
The longer-term goal is to remove the hand-written fitter entirely and learn end
to end from the reconstructed signal; supporting that would mean a different
Gymnasium contract, exposing raw images or k-space as the observation and
deriving the reward from signal agreement rather than from a fitted estimate.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:twin-summary}

This chapter delivered A1: a configurable, serialisable digital twin of the
QalibreMD Model 130 phantom built on a single \texttt{PhantomConfig} $\to$
\texttt{build\_phantom} contract. The two design decisions that matter for
the rest of the report are the independent, coarsenable background-water grid
--- the lever that makes multi-fidelity training affordable in
Chapter~\ref{ch:adaptive-rl} --- and the explicit, dependency-checked
random-phantom samplers that let each RL episode draw a fresh, plausible
phantom without touching library code. The known accuracy caveats
(placeholder PD/fiducial values, fixed $20\,^{\circ}$C tables) do not affect
the $T_1/T_2$ experiments but bound any future sim-to-real comparison. With
the twin in place, Chapter~\ref{ch:validating-komamri} turns to whether the
simulator that consumes it is numerically trustworthy.
